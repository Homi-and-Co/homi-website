The real cost of "we'll figure it out"

Ask a small business owner how the back office runs and you will often get a shrug and a smile: "We just figure it out."

It sounds like resourcefulness. Scrappy. Hands on. But in most small businesses, "we'll figure it out" is doing quiet, expensive damage, and nobody is adding it up.

Let us add it up.

What "figuring it out" actually means

When an owner says "we'll figure it out," what they usually mean is: "I will do it myself, tonight, when the customers are gone."

The quotes get written after dinner. The invoicing happens on Sunday morning. The scheduling puzzles get solved in the car between jobs. The follow-ups happen when the owner remembers them, which is sometimes, which is the whole problem.

This is not a failure of effort. Most owners who run this way work harder than anyone in their business. It is a failure of arithmetic. The math of "figuring it out" does not add up, and it gets worse every year.

Cost one: your most expensive hours

Every hour you spend on admin is an hour spent by the most expensive person in the building.

When a plumbing company owner sits down at 9 PM to build three quotes, the business is paying owner wages for work a system could do in minutes. When a boutique owner spends her Sunday re-entering inventory counts into a spreadsheet, she is doing ten-dollar-an-hour work with the person whose time determines whether the business grows.

Here is a simple exercise. Take the hours you spent last week on work that did not involve a customer, a decision only you can make, or the quality of what you sell. Now ask: if someone reliable and inexpensive handled all of it, what would you do with those hours?

Almost every owner answers the same way: more customers. Better service. Actual days off. Those are not luxuries. Those are the activities that grow revenue. Admin handled at midnight grows nothing. It only holds the line.

Cost two: the mistakes you never see

Figuring it out in real time means nothing is written down, so everything depends on memory and luck. That is where the second cost lives, and it is larger than the first.

The estimate that goes out two days late, after the customer already called someone else. The invoice that sits for three weeks because nobody has time to chase it, and then sits for three more. The double-booking that happens because the schedule lives partly in a calendar, partly on sticky notes, and partly in the owner's head. The new inquiry that gets a reply on Thursday and was ready to buy on Monday.

None of these feel like disasters. That is the trick. Each one is small, forgiven, absorbed. But small leaks compound. A missed follow-up here, a late invoice there, a customer quietly deciding you are hard to work with. Over a year, the businesses that "figure it out" lose a shocking amount of money to work that almost happened.

And there is a deeper problem: because nobody measures any of this, it looks free. The owner sees the payroll for a hire they did not make and calls that savings. The revenue that never arrived has no line item, so it never gets counted.

Cost three: the growth you never choose

Here is the heaviest cost, and the one owners talk about least.

When every new customer means more late nights, growth stops being exciting. The owner starts, without deciding to, capping the business. Not advertising the new service. Not taking the bigger account. Keeping things manageable. The business is not failing. It is protecting its owner from its own back office.

We have seen it plainly: a thriving shop that could double in a year but will not, because doubling would mean doubling the owner's admin load, and the owner is already full. That is not a capacity problem in the business. It is an admin problem wearing a capacity costume.

Ask yourself honestly: is there something you have not pursued because of the paperwork behind it? If the answer is yes, you already know what "we'll figure it out" costs.

The fix is not a hire

Here is the part that surprises people. Fixing this does not mean hiring a back office staff. Most small businesses cannot justify that, and most do not need to.

The fix is a system. Written processes that run the same way every time. Simple rules that handle confirmations, reminders, invoices, and follow-ups without a person re-typing them. A weekly briefing that puts the key numbers in front of you so you stop flying on gut feeling. Technology doing the repetitive work, a person approving what matters.

In our language, this means sorting the work into its lane: stop the work that should not exist, automate the work that follows rules, assist the work that needs judgment, and keep the human touch where it belongs. But you cannot sort work you have never written down. That is why the diagnosis comes first.

Do the math on your business

Take fifteen minutes this week and write down everything you "figured out" outside of business hours. Every email answered at night. Every invoice chased on the weekend. Every quote built after dinner. Every follow-up that happened because you remembered, and the ones that happened because you forgot.

That list is the real price of the shrug. And it is almost always bigger than the price of fixing it.
